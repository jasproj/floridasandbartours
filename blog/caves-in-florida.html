Caves in Florida

Statewide • Updated October 2026

Florida has one publicly accessible dry cave tour — Florida Caverns State Park in Marianna — and beneath it runs a hidden world of flooded limestone tunnels that extends for miles in every direction. The underwater cave systems at Devil’s Den, Ginnie Springs, Peacock Springs, Blue Grotto, Wakulla, and Madison Blue rank among the most extensive in the world and draw certified cave divers from every continent. This guide covers both surfaces: what visitors without diving certification can see, and what lies below the restriction signs for those who have earned the right to go deeper.

The One Dry Cave: Florida Caverns State Park

Florida Caverns State Park in Marianna, in the western Panhandle, is the only place in Florida where the public can walk through dry stalactite and stalagmite formations on a guided tour. The cave system formed when the Chipola River carved through limestone over thousands of years, and the resulting rooms contain the full range of speleothem formations: stalactites, stalagmites, columns, flowstone, draperies, and cave coral. The guided tour runs about 45 minutes through lit passages and is accessible to most visitors who can handle stairs and low clearances.

The park is worth a full day. Beyond the cave tour, it has a spring-fed swimming hole on the Chipola River, paddling access on the river itself, and hiking trails through the surrounding floodplain forest. The cave tour is the headliner but the spring swimming alone would justify the trip. Entry fees and tour times should be confirmed with the park directly, as they vary by season; check the Florida State Parks website.

Only public dry cave in Florida: Florida Caverns SP is commonly cited as the only state-managed dry cave open to the public for walking tours in Florida. Other cave formations in the state are either flooded (the spring cave systems) or on private land with restricted access.

Understanding the Levels: Open Water, Cavern, and Cave Diving

Before listing the underwater sites, it’s worth being precise about what the different access zones mean, because this distinction carries real safety consequences.

Open water means no overhead obstruction. A snorkeler at Devil’s Den or Ginnie Springs is in open water; they can see the surface directly above them and can ascend vertically at any time. No certification beyond basic swimming ability is needed for the surface snorkel zones at most Florida spring cave entrances.

Cavern diving is defined by the daylight zone: the diver stays within 130 feet of the cave entrance, within natural light, and at a depth from which they could ascend directly to the surface in an emergency. A cavern diver certification — available from NSS-CDS, NAUI, PADI, and TDI — qualifies a diver for this zone only.

Cave diving goes past the daylight zone into total overhead environment. The diver can no longer see the entrance, cannot ascend vertically, and must follow a continuous guideline back to open water. Cave diving requires a full cave certification, redundant equipment (two tanks or a sidemount configuration, two lights, two cutting tools, a guideline reel), and significant training. The cave certification course typically takes a full week and requires an established diving background.

The restriction signs inside Florida’s underwater caves are placed at the boundary between cavern and cave zones. They exist because people have died inside these passages after crossing them without proper preparation.

The Danger of Untrained Cave Diving

Florida’s underwater cave systems have claimed the lives of many divers, and the majority of those deaths involved divers who were not cave certified, or who were certified at a lower level than the zone they entered. The conditions that make these systems so beautiful — zero visibility possible in silt-out conditions, complex passage geometry, complete darkness beyond the entrance, no emergency ascent possible — are also the conditions that make mistakes fatal. A cave diver who loses their guideline in a silt-out with one tank and no backup light is in serious danger regardless of skill level. A recreational diver in the same situation has essentially no margin at all.

Important: Do not enter any cave passage in Florida’s underwater systems without a full cave diving certification from an accredited training organization. Open-water, advanced open-water, and cavern certifications do not qualify you to enter cave zones. Many of the fatalities in Florida’s caves involved divers who ignored restriction signs posted at the cave entrance.

Florida’s Major Cave Diving Sites

Devil’s Den (Williston)

Devil’s Den is the most visually dramatic of Florida’s spring cave sites: a collapsed sinkhole with a wooden staircase leading down to a 54-foot-deep pool inside a cave chamber. Natural light comes through the collapsed roof. Snorkelers and swimmers can use the open water area without certification; cave divers descend through the system below the pool. Fossil bones of Pleistocene megafauna have been recovered from the silt here. The site is privately owned and requires advance reservations. Full coverage in our Florida springs guide.

Ginnie Springs / Devil’s Ear (High Springs)

Ginnie Springs is a private park on the Santa Fe River that sits directly above some of the most extensively mapped cave systems in Florida. Devil’s Eye and Devil’s Ear are the best-known entrances; Ginnie Spring itself is the primary snorkel and swim site. The cave systems here are long, complex, and actively explored. The park enforces certification requirements at the cave entrances. Non-cave-certified visitors can snorkel the spring boils and enjoy the river; they simply cannot pass the restriction signs.

Peacock Springs State Park (Live Oak)

Peacock Springs in Suwannee County is one of the largest surveyed underwater cave systems in the United States. The park has several spring entrances connected underground, and cave diving here requires full certification. The site is actively used for training by cave diving instructors and is considered an advanced cave dive due to depth and passage complexity. Swimming in the springs themselves is available for non-divers; the cave entrances are restricted.

Blue Grotto (Williston)

Blue Grotto, near Williston in Levy County, is a privately operated cavern and cave diving site. The main basin is a roughly 100-foot-diameter open-water pool; below it, a cavern zone descends to about 60 feet where a cave passage begins. Cavern certification allows access to the cavern zone; cave certification is needed for the deeper passage. Non-diving visitors can sometimes observe from the surface deck; check current access policies with the site directly.

Wakulla Springs (Wakulla County)

The Wakulla Springs cave system is among the deepest and most significant in Florida, extending from the main spring boil at Wakulla Springs State Park well into the aquifer. The park itself is a fully public state park with swimming, guided glass-bottom boat tours, and one of the most impressive spring boils in the state — a vent that produces 250 million gallons per day. The cave system is restricted; access for scientific and exploration diving requires special coordination with the park authority and is not available to the general public. The park itself is an excellent destination; you simply will not be cave diving here on a day visit.

Madison Blue Spring State Park (Madison County)

Madison Blue sits on the Withlacoochee River and has a well-mapped cave system that is used for both training and recreational cave diving. The spring itself is one of the most photogenic in Florida, with an intense blue color and high visibility. Cave divers enter from the spring basin; the cave runs along the river corridor and has been surveyed for considerable distance. Non-cave-certified visitors swim in the spring area above the restriction signs.

How to See Cave Systems Without Certification

Several of Florida’s cave sites allow meaningful access to non-divers:

  • Florida Caverns SP: The walking tour is open to everyone and gives a genuine experience of a cave formation environment.
  • Devil’s Den: Snorkeling the open-water pool, you can look down into the cavern entrance and see the passage below you. The formation is genuinely impressive from the surface.
  • Ginnie Springs: The spring boil at Ginnie itself is a good snorkel; you can observe the cave entrance visually without entering it.
  • Wakulla Springs SP: The glass-bottom boat tours run over the main spring vent and allow views into the aquifer opening from the surface.

If cave diving is something you want to pursue, the path starts with a standard open-water certification, then cavern, then intro to cave, then full cave. Florida’s spring country has multiple cave diving instructors offering training, and the access to world-class sites is part of what makes it a center for the sport.

When to Visit

Florida’s cave diving sites are accessible year-round. The water temperature inside the caves is a constant 68°F regardless of season. Surface conditions affect the drive to get there but not the dive itself. The spring swimming areas at cave sites follow the same seasonal patterns as other Florida springs: summer is crowded and reservations fill quickly; spring and fall are less busy; winter is coldest but quietest.

What to Bring

  • For snorkeling at surface zones: mask, fins, wetsuit or rash guard (68°F water), water shoes
  • For the Florida Caverns dry cave tour: closed-toe shoes, layers (caves stay cool regardless of outside temperature), no open flames or smoking
  • For cave diving: everything your cave certification course specifies — this is not a packing list item but a training and equipment system

Questions People Ask

Does Florida have dry caves?

Florida has one publicly accessible dry cave tour: Florida Caverns State Park in Marianna, in the Panhandle. It is the only place in Florida where the public can walk through dry stalactite and stalagmite formations on a guided tour. Most of Florida’s cave systems are underwater.

Can you cave dive in Florida without certification?

No. Cave diving requires a dedicated cave diving certification from an organization such as NSS-CDS, NAUI, or PADI. Entering a submerged cave passage without cave certification is illegal at most sites and is extremely dangerous. Many fatalities in Florida’s cave systems have involved uncertified divers or divers with only open-water certification.

What is the difference between cavern diving and cave diving?

Cavern diving takes place in the daylight zone of a cave entrance, within 130 feet of the surface and always within sight of natural light. A cavern diver certification covers this zone. Cave diving goes beyond the daylight zone into full overhead environment, requiring additional training, redundant equipment, and a guideline reel.

What is the longest underwater cave in Florida?

Florida’s cave systems are among the most extensive in the world, but ongoing exploration means total surveyed length changes regularly. The Wakulla–Leon Sinks system in Wakulla County is among the deepest and most significant. Exact figures for the longest system should be verified with current NSS or cave diving organizations, as new passages are still being mapped.

How do I get certified for cave diving?

The standard progression is: open water certification, then cavern diver, then introductory cave, then full cave certification. Each level requires a dedicated course from an accredited instructor. NSS-CDS, NAUI, and TDI are among the recognized certifying bodies. Florida has several cave diving instructors based near the major spring systems.

Is Florida Caverns State Park worth visiting?

Yes. The dry cave tour at Florida Caverns is the only one of its kind in the state. The formations include stalactites, stalagmites, columns, and flowstone, and the tour is guided and well-maintained. The park also has a spring-fed swimming area, hiking trails, and canoe access on the Chipola River.

Can non-divers visit cave systems in Florida?

Yes. At Devil’s Den and at Ginnie Springs, the open-water swimming area above the cave entrance is open to snorkelers without any certification. You can see the cave entrance and the beginning of the passage from the surface. Entering the cave itself requires proper certification. Florida Caverns State Park offers dry cave tours requiring no diving at all.
