Florida Springs: The Complete Guide to Swimming Holes

Statewide • Updated October 2026

Florida has more first-magnitude springs than anywhere else on Earth. More than 700 natural springs dot the state, and hundreds of them are open for swimming, snorkeling, kayaking, and tubing. The water temperature holds steady at around 68–72°F year-round, making these springs refreshing in summer and surprisingly mild in winter. This guide covers the fifteen most significant swimming holes and spring parks in Florida, organized by region, with practical information on reservations, manatee rules, and safety.

Whether you want to float down the crystal-clear Ichetucknee River, snorkel with manatees in Crystal River, watch the mermaids at Weeki Wachee, or dive the cavern at Devil’s Den, this is where to start.

What Makes Florida Springs Different

Florida’s springs are not rivers or lakes. They are the surface expression of the Floridan Aquifer, an immense limestone reservoir that underlies most of the state. Rainwater percolates through the porous limestone over decades, filtering clean, and emerges at the surface wherever a vent opens in the rock. The process produces water of extraordinary clarity — sometimes visibility exceeds 100 feet in the main boils — and the constant temperature that has nothing to do with seasons.

A spring is classified as first-magnitude if it discharges at least 100 cubic feet of water per second. Florida has more of these high-volume springs than any other place on Earth, and they are concentrated mostly in the north-central part of the state in what hydrologists call the Spring Coast. The same aquifer system creates the underwater cave networks that draw certified cave divers from around the world. See our separate guide to caves in Florida if cave diving is on your list.

North Central Florida: The Heart of Spring Country

Ginnie Springs

Ginnie Springs, on the Santa Fe River in Gilchrist County near High Springs, is a privately owned park with seven springs and one of the highest concentrations of cave diving access points in the United States. The main Ginnie Spring boil is large and clear, and swimmers and snorkelers share the water comfortably. Devil’s Eye and Devil’s Ear are within the same park and are among the most famous cave diving entrances in Florida. Non-divers can access the swimming area without cave certification; going beyond the restriction signs requires full cave diving credentials.

Ichetucknee Springs State Park

Ichetucknee is the closest thing Florida has to a bucket-list natural experience that nearly anyone can do. The Ichetucknee River runs roughly six miles from the head spring to the take-out at the US 27 bridge, fed by nine springs along its length. Tubers float the upper section (about 3.5 miles) or the full run. The water is clear enough to see the river bottom in fifteen feet, and the vegetation lining the banks is lush and undisturbed. Snorkelers regularly see turtles, largemouth bass, and the occasional manatee in winter. The park caps daily visitor numbers and sells timed entry tickets — book ahead on weekends and all of summer.

Madison Blue Spring State Park

Madison Blue is a compact, uncrowded state park on the Withlacoochee River in Madison County. The main spring vent is a vivid cobalt blue and among the most photogenic in the state. It is also a popular cave diving destination with a well-mapped system extending well into the aquifer. Swimmers access the spring basin from the boardwalk; divers enter from the same area with proper certification.

Gilchrist Blue Spring State Park and Fanning Springs

Gilchrist Blue is one of the newer state parks in the spring corridor, opened on the Santa Fe River near Bell. It is less crowded than Ginnie Springs and offers excellent swimming and snorkeling in a well-maintained setting. Fanning Springs State Park, a few miles east on the Suwannee River, is a smaller spring with a large swimming area and easy access. Both make good alternatives when the more popular springs reach capacity.

Ocala National Forest: Three Springs Worth Knowing

Alexander Springs

Alexander Springs in the Ocala National Forest pumps more than 76 million gallons per day, making it one of the larger second-magnitude springs in the forest. The swimming area is a natural pool formed by the spring boil and the shallow headwaters of Alexander Creek, which paddlers can explore downstream. The forest campground here is popular; day visitors should arrive early in summer.

Juniper Springs

Juniper Springs has one of the best canoe runs in Florida: a seven-mile paddle through Juniper Creek from the spring to the take-out at the trailhead, winding through dense subtropical forest. The swimming hole at the headspring is smaller than Alexander but clear and cold. Timed entry tickets are required and sell out on summer weekends.

Silver Glen Springs

Silver Glen, on the western edge of Lake George in the Ocala National Forest, is a large boil spring with warm clear water and boat access from the lake. It is accessible by car and by boat and is particularly popular with anglers and paddlers. The spring run connects to Lake George, so boat traffic can be heavy on weekends.

West-Central Florida Springs

Kelly Park / Rock Springs Run

Rock Springs at Kelly Park in Orange County is the best free-floating tube run in Florida without formal state park infrastructure. The run is about three miles and entirely spring-fed. Orange County Parks runs Kelly Park; it opens early and closes when the parking lot fills, which on summer weekends can happen before 9 a.m. Arrive at opening, bring a tube, and drift the run. The water temperature holds around 68°F even in August.

Weeki Wachee Springs State Park

Weeki Wachee is both a working state park and an old-Florida institution. Since 1947, performers in mermaid tails have staged underwater shows in the natural spring theater, breathing from air hoses while millions of gallons of water push past them. There is a full waterpark and river boat tour beyond the mermaid show, plus a wilderness kayak launch on the Weeki Wachee River. For the full story, see our piece on the mermaids of Weeki Wachee.

Three Sisters Springs and Crystal River National Wildlife Refuge

Three Sisters Springs, managed by the US Fish and Wildlife Service inside the Crystal River National Wildlife Refuge, is the most important manatee sanctuary on Florida’s Gulf Coast. In winter, West Indian manatees congregate in the warm spring water by the hundreds. Swimming with manatees here is legal during designated manatee seasons with a licensed guide who follows USFWS passive observation rules — no touching, no chasing, no flash photography. During the warm months when manatees are not present, the crystal-clear water and abundant fish life still make it worth a visit. Guided snorkel tours depart from Crystal River and Kings Bay. For more on where and when to find manatees, see our Florida manatee tours guide.

Central Florida Springs

Wekiwa Springs State Park

Wekiwa Springs is twenty minutes north of Orlando and feels nothing like its surroundings. The spring feeds a large clear swimming area inside a mature oak and longleaf pine forest. Paddlers launch onto Wekiva River for a half-day or full-day float to Rock Springs Run, passing Florida wildlife along both banks. The park is heavily used because of its proximity to Orlando; book entry reservations as far ahead as the system allows.

Blue Spring State Park

Blue Spring in Orange City is Florida’s most famous manatee refuge. The St. Johns River near here drops below 68°F in winter, and manatees pour into the warm spring run — more than 700 were recorded in a single count during the 2022–23 season. When manatees are present, the spring run is closed to swimmers to protect the animals; this closure runs approximately November 15 through March 31, though the exact dates vary with water temperature and manatee activity. Confirm with the park before you visit. Outside of manatee season, the spring is open for swimming and snorkeling. The boardwalk along the run is open year-round and gives excellent views of manatees in winter.

Blue Spring swim closure: Swimming is prohibited in the run when manatees are present, typically November 15 through March 31. Dates can extend if manatees remain longer than usual. Check the park’s official page before you go.

Southwest Florida Springs

Rainbow Springs State Park

Rainbow Springs near Dunnellon in Marion County is the headwater of the Rainbow River, one of the most beautiful spring runs in the state. The main swimming area is at the KP Hole County Park at the south end (a separate facility from the state park), where tubers take out after a four-mile float. The spring park itself offers swimming and kayak and canoe rental. Rainbow Springs is also notable for the man-made waterfalls constructed in the 1960s when the site operated as a commercial attraction — they are still maintained and worth seeing, but they are not natural.

Devil’s Den

Devil’s Den is a prehistoric underground spring inside a dry cave in Levy County near Williston. The roof of the cave collapsed thousands of years ago, leaving a sky opening above a 54-foot-deep pool with extraordinary visibility. Snorkelers reach the water down a wooden staircase; cave divers descend through the cavern entrance at the bottom of the pool into a mapped cave system. No cave certification is needed to snorkel the open water, but going beyond the line and lights requires full cave credentials. Fossil bones of extinct Pleistocene animals have been found in the silted floor. Reservations are required; Devil’s Den is privately owned and managed.

Devil’s Den shares geography with Ginnie Springs and is part of the same limestone karst system that underlies Florida’s cave diving world. Read more in our complete guide to caves in Florida.

Planning Your Visit

When to Go

Spring (March through May) offers the best combination: air temperatures are warm enough for comfortable swimming, crowds are smaller than summer, and flowers line the spring runs. Fall (September through November) is similar. Summer brings the largest crowds and the most capacity closures; many parks now sell out online days in advance. Winter is the right season for manatee watching at Blue Spring and Crystal River, though the 68–72°F water feels genuinely cold when the air is in the 50s.

Reservations and Capacity Caps

Florida State Parks uses an online reservation system for day-use entry at the most popular springs. Ichetucknee, Wekiwa, Blue Spring, Rainbow Springs, Alexander Springs, and Juniper Springs all use timed-entry or reservation systems at various times of year. Check the Florida State Parks website for each park before visiting; weekend reservations in summer often sell out within minutes of opening. Ginnie Springs and Devil’s Den are private and take reservations through their own systems.

What to Bring

  • Wetsuit or rash guard: 68–72°F is colder than it sounds for extended snorkeling; a 3mm shorty wetsuit makes a long session comfortable.
  • Fins and mask: Rental gear is available at most springs but limited; bring your own if possible.
  • Water shoes: The limestone bottom at many springs is sharp.
  • Life jacket: Required for non-swimmers in some parks; the spring run currents are mild but persistent.
  • Reef-safe sunscreen: Oxybenzone and octinoxate harm spring ecosystems; many parks require mineral sunscreen.
  • Cash or credit: Entry fees vary from free (some county parks) to $15–20 per vehicle for state parks.

Safety

The main swimming areas at state parks are well-managed and generally safe for swimmers of all levels. The hazards worth knowing: spring runs can have moderate currents that exhaust weak swimmers; the cave entrances visible at many springs are genuinely dangerous without proper certification — cave diving is a specialty requiring dedicated training, equipment, and experience, and people without it have died inside Florida’s cave systems; and the cold water causes rapid loss of body heat. Stay within designated swim areas, observe all restriction lines, and do not attempt to enter any cave or cavern passage without appropriate certification.

Questions People Ask

What temperature are Florida springs?

Most of Florida’s major springs maintain a constant temperature of around 68–72°F year-round. This feels refreshing in summer and cold in winter — bring a wetsuit if you are sensitive to temperature.

When is the best time to visit Florida springs?

Spring (March–May) and fall (September–November) offer the best combination of warm air and manageable crowds. Summer is the busiest season; many springs now require advance reservations and fill up fast. Winter is ideal for seeing manatees at Blue Spring and Crystal River.

Which Florida springs require reservations?

Many popular springs now require advance reservations through the Florida State Parks reservation system, including Ichetucknee, Wekiwa, Blue Spring, Rainbow Springs, Alexander, and Juniper. Ginnie Springs and Devil’s Den are privately owned and managed separately. Check each park’s official page before visiting.

Can you swim with manatees at Florida springs?

The main location for swimming with manatees is Crystal River / Three Sisters Springs in Citrus County, which offers guided snorkel tours during manatee season (roughly November through March). Blue Spring State Park hosts large numbers of manatees but does not allow swimming when manatees are present — typically November 15 through March 31; confirm current dates with the park.

What is a first-magnitude spring?

A first-magnitude spring discharges at least 100 cubic feet of water per second. Florida has more documented first-magnitude springs than any other region on Earth, concentrated mostly in the north-central part of the state.

Are Florida springs safe to swim in?

The main swimming areas at state parks and private spring parks are well-maintained and generally safe. Hazards include strong currents inside spring runs, cave systems (which require certification and equipment), cold shock from the 68–72°F water, and boat traffic on shared waterways. Stay within designated swim areas unless you are qualified for open-water or cave diving.

Do you need a permit to snorkel at Florida springs?

No special permit is needed for snorkeling at designated swimming springs. You do need an entry ticket or day-use fee for state parks. Some springs, including Three Sisters Springs, require a guided tour or specific permit to enter the swim area during manatee season.

What is the most popular spring in Florida?

Ichetucknee Springs and Ginnie Springs are among the most visited, particularly for tubing and snorkeling. Blue Spring is Florida’s most popular manatee-viewing spot. Rainbow Springs is known for its clarity and paddling. All can reach capacity on summer weekends — arrive early or reserve ahead.
