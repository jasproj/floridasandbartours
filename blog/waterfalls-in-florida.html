Waterfalls in Florida

Statewide • Updated October 2026

Florida is one of the flattest states in the country. Most of it sits only a few feet above sea level, and the highest natural point in the state — Britton Hill in Walton County — rises just 345 feet. Honest expectations matter when you are searching for waterfalls here: these are not Niagara or Yosemite. But genuine waterfalls do exist in Florida, and two of them are genuinely worth a detour. Falling Waters State Park near Chipley has the state’s tallest waterfall, dropping roughly 74 feet into a sinkhole. Big Shoals on the Suwannee River has Florida’s most significant whitewater. The others on this list round out a complete picture of what the state actually offers.

Falling Waters State Park: Florida’s Tallest Waterfall

Falling Waters State Park outside Chipley in Washington County, in the Panhandle, is the site of Florida’s tallest waterfall. A stream flows over the lip of a cylindrical sinkhole and drops approximately 74 feet to the bottom of a roughly 100-foot-deep pit — figures from Florida State Parks documentation, which should be verified with the park directly. The base of the pit is not visible from the top; the waterfall simply disappears into the earth.

The experience is genuine. You walk a short boardwalk trail through the park and arrive at the sinkhole rim, where a viewing platform gives a clear look at the waterfall and the depth of the pit. The sound of the water hitting the bottom echoes up. The pit is fenced and not accessible from below. Other sinkholes in the park can be viewed from the trail, and there is a swimming lake with a sand beach that many visitors find the more enjoyable part of the day.

Practical note: Falling Waters is in the Panhandle, about 80 miles east of Pensacola and an hour north of Panama City Beach. It makes a logical day stop on a Panhandle road trip but is out of the way as a standalone destination from central or south Florida.

The waterfall flows most reliably in winter and spring when rainfall keeps the stream topped up. Summer afternoon thunderstorms refill it quickly, but drought conditions in late summer can reduce the flow to a trickle. Check with the park if you are making a specific trip for the waterfall.

Rainbow Springs State Park: Scenic But Man-Made

Rainbow Springs State Park near Dunnellon has multiple tiered waterfalls cascading over limestone and tropical plantings. They look natural and photograph beautifully. They are not natural. The falls were constructed in the 1960s when Rainbow Springs operated as a commercial tourist attraction. The waterfalls, along with trained bird shows and submarine boat rides, were part of the park’s entertainment program.

This doesn’t make them unpleasant to visit — they are well-maintained and genuinely scenic, and the surrounding landscape of the natural Rainbow Springs and the crystal-clear Rainbow River is worth the trip for its own reasons. Just know what you are looking at. The spring and the river are entirely natural; the waterfalls are not. For more on the springs and swimming here, see our complete Florida springs guide.

Steinhatchee Falls

Steinhatchee Falls on the Steinhatchee River, straddling the Taylor and Dixie county lines, is a broad, shallow cascade over exposed limestone bedrock rather than a dramatic drop. The falls extend roughly 60 feet across the river and drop about two feet in a series of steps. In low-water conditions, which are typical in the dry season, you can wade across the falls and stand in the middle of the river as the water rushes around your ankles. In high water the river submerges the limestone and the falls flatten out.

Steinhatchee is a small fishing town known for scalloping in summer and redfish in fall. The falls are a local curiosity worth a stop if you are in the area, but they are not a destination in themselves. Access is informal; park near the SR 51 bridge and walk down the bank.

Big Shoals State Park: Florida’s Best Whitewater

Big Shoals on the Suwannee River near White Springs in Hamilton County is not a waterfall in the strict sense — there is no vertical drop — but it is Florida’s most significant whitewater feature and worth including in any honest list of dramatic water in the state. The shoals are a series of exposed limestone ledges and boulders extending about a third of a mile across the Suwannee, creating Class III rapids in moderate-to-high water conditions.

The Suwannee drains a large watershed, and water levels here vary significantly with rainfall. In high water the rapids are genuinely challenging for canoeists and kayakers; in low water the shoals expose more rock and the whitewater diminishes. The state park has a viewing platform above the river where you can observe the rapids without getting on the water. Canoe access is available upstream for those who want to run the shoals, though the skill required in high water is real — assess the conditions before committing a loaded canoe.

Big Shoals in context: By national standards, Class III is a moderate rapid. By Florida standards, it is remarkable — nearly nothing else in the state comes close. If you kayak or canoe and you are visiting north Florida, it is worth a look.

Devil’s Millhopper Geological State Park

Devil’s Millhopper in Gainesville is a large, bowl-shaped sinkhole about 120 feet deep and 500 feet wide, with a boardwalk staircase of over 200 steps descending to the bottom. Numerous small streams emerge from cracks in the walls and flow down the sides of the sinkhole to the floor, where they disappear into the limestone below. After significant rainfall, these seeps become visible trickles and small cascades down the moss-covered walls. During dry conditions, there may be little visible water at all.

Calling Devil’s Millhopper a waterfall site is generous; calling it a sinkhole with interesting hydrology is accurate. The reason to visit is the geology and the microclimate: the bottom of the sinkhole is measurably cooler than the rim and supports subtropical ferns and plants that don’t normally grow in this part of Florida. Fossil marine sediment exposed in the sinkhole walls gives the site its age. It is a short, interesting stop for anyone with a geology or natural history interest, particularly in combination with the springs at nearby Wekiwa or Ichetucknee.

What About South and Central Florida?

Central and south Florida have no natural waterfalls worth visiting. The landscape is too flat. Some artificial falls exist at theme parks, resorts, and botanical gardens, but these are constructed features with no natural geology behind them. If someone on social media is posting a “waterfall in Miami” or a “waterfall in Orlando,” they are either showing a theme park structure or something very small in a botanical garden. The genuine waterfall sites in Florida are all in the north and the Panhandle.

How Waterfalls Fit into a Florida Trip

The waterfall sites in Florida work best as part of a broader north Florida itinerary that includes the spring systems and state parks. Falling Waters is a natural add-on to a trip through the Panhandle. Big Shoals fits well with a paddling trip on the Suwannee. Devil’s Millhopper combines with a visit to Ichetucknee Springs or Ginnie Springs in the north-central spring corridor.

For the most dramatic water experiences in Florida, the springs are more visually impressive than any of the waterfalls. Ichetucknee’s clarity, the blue of Ginnie, and the volume of Rainbow Springs are genuinely spectacular. See our complete guide to Florida springs for full coverage.

Planning Your Visit

When to Go

Late fall through spring (October through April) is generally best for all of these sites. Falling Waters flows most reliably after winter and spring rains. Steinhatchee Falls is most accessible when the river is at a moderate level. Big Shoals has the best whitewater after heavy rain, though flash floods are possible — check river gauges before paddling. Devil’s Millhopper has the most visible seeps after rain but is worth visiting any time for the sinkhole itself. Avoid peak summer afternoons at outdoor sites: afternoon thunderstorms are the rule from May through September and the lightning risk at exposed limestone sites is real.

What to Bring

  • Comfortable walking shoes with traction (wet limestone is slippery)
  • Water and snacks (services are limited at these parks)
  • Bug spray (north Florida in summer is mosquito territory)
  • Camera (the sinkhole sites and the Suwannee landscape photograph well)
  • Entry fee — state parks charge a day-use fee; check the Florida State Parks website for current rates

Questions People Ask

Does Florida have waterfalls?

Yes, though they are modest compared to mountainous states. Florida’s tallest waterfall is at Falling Waters State Park near Chipley, where water drops approximately 74 feet into a cylindrical sinkhole. Big Shoals on the Suwannee River is Florida’s best whitewater. Rainbow Springs has scenic waterfalls, but they are man-made.

What is the tallest waterfall in Florida?

Falling Waters State Park near Chipley in Washington County has Florida’s tallest waterfall. Water flows over the rim of a sinkhole and drops approximately 74 feet (per Florida State Parks documentation) to the bottom of the 100-foot-deep cylindrical pit. Verify current figures with the park authority.

Are the waterfalls at Rainbow Springs natural?

No. The waterfalls at Rainbow Springs State Park were constructed in the 1960s when the site operated as a commercial attraction called Rainbow Springs. They are scenic and well-maintained but man-made. The spring itself and the Rainbow River are entirely natural.

Where are the waterfalls in central Florida?

Central Florida has very few waterfalls. Devil’s Millhopper Geological State Park in Gainesville has small seeps and trickles inside a large sinkhole that could be called falls after rain. The main waterfall sites are in north Florida (Falling Waters, Steinhatchee Falls, Big Shoals) and the Panhandle.

Can you swim at Florida waterfalls?

At Falling Waters, swimming is not available at the waterfall itself — you observe it from a viewing platform. Steinhatchee Falls can be waded and accessed on foot, and is popular for wading in low-water season. Big Shoals is a rapids area on the Suwannee River; swimming is possible in calmer sections. Check each site for current conditions and restrictions.

When is the best time to visit Florida waterfalls?

Late fall through spring (October through April) is generally best. Summer brings heavy afternoon rain that can cause flash flooding at sinkhole sites. Steinhatchee Falls is most accessible when the river is at a comfortable wading depth. Big Shoals is best after moderate rain when there is enough water for visible whitewater but not so much as to flood the viewing access.

Is Devil’s Millhopper worth visiting?

Yes, for its geology. Devil’s Millhopper is a 120-foot-deep, 500-foot-wide sinkhole in Gainesville with a boardwalk staircase to the bottom. Small seeps emerge from the walls after rain. The main reason to visit is the sinkhole itself and the subtropical microclimate at the bottom, not a dramatic waterfall.

What are Florida’s best whitewater rapids?

Big Shoals on the Suwannee River near White Springs is Florida’s most significant whitewater. It is rated Class III in higher water conditions. It is modest by national standards but genuinely exciting by Florida standards. Access is through Big Shoals State Park.
