15 Things to Do in Naples FL

Collier County, Florida • Updated August 2026

Naples is the polished side of the Gulf Coast. Where Tampa Bay destinations trade on spring-break energy, Naples trades on white sand, quiet money, and some of the best sunsets in the state. The town sits on the southwest coast, backed by the Everglades and fronted by calm, warm Gulf water. It's a place that works for couples looking for a slow-paced beach trip and families who want nature without the crowds.

Here's what's actually worth doing — from the beaches and the pier to the Everglades trips most visitors don't know about.

Getting oriented: Naples is about 2 hours west of Miami via Alligator Alley (I-75) and 40 minutes south of Fort Myers. The beach area runs along the Gulf, downtown is a few blocks inland, and the Everglades begin at the eastern edge of town.

1. Naples Pier at Sunset

The Naples Pier is the town's landmark — a 1,000-foot fishing pier that juts into the Gulf from the south end of 12th Avenue South. It was originally built in 1888 and has been rebuilt several times after hurricanes. The real draw is sunset: people gather on the beach and the pier every evening to watch the sun drop into the Gulf, and on clear nights the sky turns orange and pink in a way that justifies Naples' reputation as a sunset destination. Fishing from the pier is free — no license required. Dolphins cruise the pilings regularly.

2. Naples Beach (Lowdermilk Park)

Lowdermilk Beach Park is the most accessible public beach in Naples proper. It has parking (metered), restrooms, showers, a playground, volleyball courts, and a concession stand. The sand is white and fine, the water is warm and calm, and it never feels as packed as Clearwater or Siesta Key. For a quieter stretch, walk south from Lowdermilk toward the pier — the sand is the same, the crowds thin, and it's one of the most pleasant beach walks on the Gulf Coast.

3. Clam Pass Beach Park

Clam Pass is a different kind of beach experience. You park at a lot off Seagate Drive and take a free tram (or walk) along a 3/4-mile boardwalk through a mangrove forest before it opens onto a wide, quiet beach. The boardwalk itself is worth it — you'll see herons, egrets, and fiddler crabs. The beach is less developed than Lowdermilk: there's a small concession and chair rentals, but the feel is more natural. At low tide, the tidal pools near the clam pass inlet are a good spot for kids to explore.

4. Fifth Avenue South

Fifth Avenue South is downtown Naples' main drag — a walkable stretch of restaurants, galleries, boutiques, and sidewalk cafes running from the bay to roughly Goodlette-Frank Road. It's upscale but not stuffy. Dinner at a patio table on Fifth Avenue on a warm winter evening is one of Naples' signature experiences. The restaurants range from high-end seafood (USS Nemo, The Bay House) to reliable Italian and sushi. On Wednesday evenings in season, the city closes streets for live music events.

5. Third Street South

Third Street South is Fifth Avenue's quieter, more residential counterpart a few blocks away. Art galleries, independent shops, and some of Naples' best restaurants cluster around a courtyard setting with fountains and landscaping. The Saturday morning farmers market (October through April) is a local institution — produce, baked goods, flowers, and food stalls. The Fleischmann Park end connects to the neighborhoods south of downtown where banyan trees arch over the streets.

6. Everglades Day Trips

Naples is the western gateway to the Everglades, and most visitors don't realize how close they are. Everglades City is about 45 minutes southeast, and it's the launch point for Ten Thousand Islands boat tours, kayak trips, and airboat rides through the mangrove backcountry. The Shark Valley entrance to Everglades National Park is about 90 minutes via US-41 (Tamiami Trail) — rent bikes or take the tram to the observation tower. Guided kayak tours through the mangrove tunnels are a half-day commitment and one of the best outdoor experiences in South Florida.

7. Naples Botanical Garden

A 170-acre garden that opened in 2009 and has become one of the best botanical gardens in the Southeast. The gardens are organized by region — Brazilian, Caribbean, Asian, Florida native — and the landscape architecture is excellent. The children's garden has a butterfly house and water play area. It's air-conditioned-adjacent in the sense that the tree canopy keeps you cool, but bring water in the summer. The cultivar garden and the lake trail loop are highlights.

8. Naples Zoo at Caribbean Gardens

A small, well-run zoo set in a historic tropical garden dating to 1919. The collection includes giraffes, lions, bears, alligators, red pandas, and a walk-through aviary. The boat ride through islands of primates (howler monkeys, lemurs, spider monkeys) is unique to this zoo. It's compact enough to see everything in two to three hours, which is a plus with younger kids. The giraffe feeding experience is popular and worth the small extra fee.

9. Ten Thousand Islands Boat Tours

The Ten Thousand Islands are a maze of mangrove islands stretching south of Naples into Everglades National Park. Guided boat tours run out of Everglades City and Goodland (on Marco Island's south end). You'll see dolphins, manatees, sea turtles, ospreys, and bald eagles depending on the season. Some tours include a stop on a shell island for shelling and swimming. Kayak tours through the mangrove tunnels are the more intimate version — you paddle through root systems and tidal creeks where the powerboats can't go.

10. Delnor-Wiggins Pass State Park

A mile-long barrier island beach at the north end of Naples that consistently ranks among the best beaches in Florida. The sand is white, the water is clear, and the park limits the number of visitors (once the lot fills, they close the gate). There's good snorkeling near the jetty at the north tip, especially in calm conditions. The park also has a boat ramp and a nature trail through the coastal hammock. It's what a Florida beach looked like before the high-rises went up.

11. Tin City and the Naples Bayfront

Tin City is a waterfront shopping and dining complex in old tin-roofed fishing shacks along Naples Bay. It's touristy, but it's also where many of the dolphin cruise and sunset sail operators dock. The bayfront area — including Bayfront Place across the street — has restaurants with water views, a small boutique district, and the departure point for Gordon River cruises. If you're booking a boat tour in Naples, chances are it leaves from here.

12. Corkscrew Swamp Sanctuary

An Audubon sanctuary about 30 minutes northeast of Naples with a 2.25-mile boardwalk through one of the last old-growth bald cypress forests in the world. Some of the trees are over 500 years old. The boardwalk passes through cypress forest, wet prairie, and marsh — birding is exceptional, especially during nesting season (December through April) when wood storks and other wading birds breed here. It's quiet, slow, and completely different from the beach. Allow two to three hours.

13. Marco Island Day Trip

Marco Island is 20 minutes south of Naples and feels like a different world — a large barrier island with a wide crescent beach (Residents' Beach requires a pass, but Tigertail Beach is public), shelling on the south end, and a quieter, more residential atmosphere. The bridge from the mainland is the only way on and off. It's a solid half-day trip for the beach and lunch, or combine it with a Ten Thousand Islands boat tour departing from Goodland on the island's south side.

14. Shelling on Keewaydin Island

Keewaydin is a barrier island south of Naples accessible only by boat — no bridge, no cars, no development. Shelling here rivals Sanibel in quality because far fewer people make the trip. The island is about 8 miles long with white sand beaches, clear water, and piles of shells at the tide line. Several operators run boat shuttles from Naples and Marco Island. Bring everything you need (water, sunscreen, snacks) because there are no facilities. Low tide, early morning, after a storm — that's the shelling trifecta.

15. Golf

Naples calls itself the golf capital of the world, and the numbers back it up — more golf holes per capita than anywhere else in the US. Most of the premier courses are private, but there are excellent public and semi-private options. Tiburon Golf Club (two Greg Norman courses), Naples Beach Hotel & Golf Club, and Lely Resort's Flamingo Island Course are all open to the public. The Valencia Golf & Country Club offers competitive day rates. Peak season is January through March when snowbirds and visitors fill tee times, so book well ahead.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Naples FL worth visiting?

Yes. Naples has some of the best beaches on the Gulf Coast, a charming downtown with excellent restaurants, and the Everglades are right next door. It's quieter and more upscale than the Tampa Bay area or the Keys, which is the whole point — if you want polish and nature without the spring-break energy, Naples delivers.

What is Naples Florida known for?

White sand beaches, the Naples Pier, upscale shopping on Fifth Avenue and Third Street South, world-class golf, and access to the Everglades and Ten Thousand Islands. It's also a strong shelling destination — Keewaydin Island and the beaches south of town rival Sanibel for quality.

When is the best time to visit Naples FL?

December through April is peak season — warm and dry with the most activity downtown and on the water. January through March is the busiest. May and November are shoulder months with good weather and lower rates. Summer is hot, humid, and quiet, but hotel prices drop 30-50%.

Is Naples good for families?

Yes. Calm beaches, the Naples Zoo, Naples Botanical Garden, Everglades boat tours, and shelling trips all work for kids. The town is walkable and safe, and the beach parks (Clam Pass, Delnor-Wiggins) have good facilities.

How far is Naples from Miami?

About 125 miles, roughly two hours by car across Alligator Alley (I-75). From Fort Myers, it's 40 minutes south. From Tampa, about 2.5 hours via I-75.
