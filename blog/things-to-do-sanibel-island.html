14 Things to Do on Sanibel Island

Lee County, Florida • Updated August 2026

Sanibel Island sits off the southwest Florida coast near Fort Myers, connected by a three-mile causeway and governed by a simple rule: nothing taller than the palm trees. There are no high-rises, no chain restaurants, no traffic lights, and no boardwalk. What there is: some of the best shelling in the world, a national wildlife refuge that covers a third of the island, and a pace that feels like Florida did decades ago.

Sanibel took a direct hit from Hurricane Ian in 2022. The causeway was rebuilt, the businesses have come back, and the beaches are open. Some scars remain, but the island's character didn't change. Here's what's worth doing.

Getting oriented: Sanibel is reached via the Sanibel Causeway ($6 toll each way) from the mainland near Fort Myers. Periwinkle Way is the main road. The lighthouse is at the east tip, Captiva Island connects at the west end, and the wildlife refuge runs through the middle. From Fort Myers airport (RSW), it's about 30 minutes to the causeway.

1. Shelling

Sanibel is the shelling capital of the Western Hemisphere, and the geology explains why. The island runs east-west (most Florida barrier islands run north-south), which means it acts like a net catching shells carried north by Gulf currents. The "Sanibel Stoop" — bent at the waist, scanning the tide line — is the island's unofficial posture. The best spots are Bowman's Beach (less crowded, better picking), Blind Pass Beach (where shells funnel through the channel between Sanibel and Captiva), and the east end near the lighthouse after a storm. Low tide, early morning, winter months after strong west winds — that's when the best shells wash up. Common finds include lightning whelks, fighting conchs, king's crowns, lettered olives, and — if you're lucky — a junonia.

2. J.N. "Ding" Darling National Wildlife Refuge

This 6,400-acre refuge covers roughly a third of Sanibel Island and is one of the largest undeveloped mangrove ecosystems in the US. Wildlife Drive is a 4-mile one-way road through the refuge — drive it, bike it, or walk it. At low tide, the mudflats fill with roseate spoonbills, great blue herons, white pelicans, ibis, and dozens of other species. Alligators bask on the banks. Manatees appear in the deeper channels in winter. The Indigo Trail boardwalk loop is a short walk through mangroves. The visitor center has exhibits and maps. Go early in the morning or late afternoon for the best wildlife viewing — and check the tide chart, because low tide concentrates the birds.

3. Lighthouse Beach Park

The Sanibel Lighthouse sits at the island's east tip — a brown iron-skeleton tower built in 1884, still active. The park around it has a fishing pier (T-dock), a beach, picnic tables, and restrooms. The beach here faces both the Gulf and San Carlos Bay, so you get two different shorelines. Shelling is good on the Gulf side after storms. The pier is a popular fishing spot — no license needed on the pier itself. Sunrise here, with the lighthouse silhouetted against the sky, is one of the classic Sanibel photos.

4. Bowman's Beach

Bowman's Beach is on the north side of the island, away from the resort areas. You park, walk across a bridge over a bayou, and reach a beach that feels more remote than it is. The shelling is consistently the best on Sanibel — fewer people walk this stretch, and the shells pile up at the tide line. There's a playground, picnic area, and outdoor showers, but no food or rental concessions. Bring what you need. At low tide, walk west along the beach toward Blind Pass for the best picking. The sand here is white and the water is clear.

5. Biking the Island

Sanibel has over 25 miles of paved, shared-use paths that run the length of the island — completely separated from the roads. You can bike from the lighthouse to Captiva without ever riding in traffic. The path follows Periwinkle Way, then branches to the refuge, the beaches, and through residential neighborhoods shaded by live oaks and sea grapes. Bike rentals are available at several shops on Periwinkle Way (Billy's Bikes is the best-known). A bike is honestly the best way to get around the island — parking at the beaches is limited, and the paths connect everything.

6. Bailey-Matthews National Shell Museum

The only museum in the US devoted entirely to shells and mollusks. It was already a Sanibel landmark before Ian, and it reopened with expanded exhibits including a live mollusk aquarium where you can see the animals that make the shells you're collecting on the beach. The Great Hall of Shells has specimens from around the world. If you're shelling on Sanibel, this museum helps you identify what you're finding and understand why Sanibel's geography makes it a global shelling hotspot. Allow about an hour and a half.

7. Captiva Island

Captiva is Sanibel's smaller, quieter neighbor connected at Blind Pass on the west end. It's about 5 miles long with a handful of resorts, restaurants, and the Captiva Village shopping area. The beach at Alison Hagerup Beach Park is small but pretty, and the sunset view from the north end of Captiva is one of the best in Florida. The Mucky Duck and RC Otter's are the go-to restaurants. Captiva is also the departure point for boat trips to Cayo Costa State Park and the outer islands (see below). It takes about 20 minutes to drive from the Sanibel causeway to Captiva Village.

8. Cayo Costa State Park

Cayo Costa is a barrier island north of Captiva, accessible only by boat or ferry. It's 9 miles of undeveloped beach, pine forest, mangroves, and some of the best shelling in Florida — better than Sanibel, some say, because far fewer people make the trip. The ferry runs from Captiva (Tropic Star Cruises). There are basic cabins and tent camping, but most visitors come for the day. Bring everything: water, food, sunscreen. There's no concession. The beach on the Gulf side is wide, white, and often empty. Dolphins and manatees frequent the bay side.

9. Kayaking and Paddleboarding

The mangrove waterways, tidal creeks, and bay shoreline around Sanibel are ideal for paddling. Tarpon Bay Explorers (inside the Ding Darling refuge) runs guided kayak tours through mangrove tunnels where manatees, dolphins, and wading birds are common. The Commodore Creek canoe trail is a marked paddling route through the refuge. For open-water paddling, launch from Bowman's Beach or the causeway islands. SUP rentals are available at several spots. The water is flat, warm, and shallow — even beginners are comfortable here.

10. Sanibel Historical Museum & Village

A cluster of restored historic buildings on Dunlop Road near city hall — a schoolhouse, a post office, a packing house, a tea room, and several homes, all dating from the late 1800s and early 1900s. The displays tell the story of Sanibel before the causeway was built in 1963, when the only way to the island was by boat. It's small and takes about an hour, but it gives you the context for why Sanibel chose to develop the way it did — height limits, no chains, conservation-first.

11. Fishing

Sanibel and Captiva sit in some of the most productive fishing waters on the Gulf Coast. Inshore guides work the flats, mangrove shorelines, and oyster bars for snook, redfish, spotted seatrout, and tarpon (tarpon season runs May through August, and they stack up in the passes around Sanibel). Offshore charters run to reefs and wrecks for grouper, snapper, and kingfish. The Sanibel Pier and the Blind Pass bridge are popular shore-fishing spots. The causeway islands between the mainland and Sanibel are a wade-fishing and kayak-fishing destination in their own right — redfish and trout in the grass flats on a falling tide.

12. Periwinkle Way Shopping and Dining

Periwinkle Way is Sanibel's main commercial street — but it doesn't look like one. The businesses are tucked into low-rise buildings behind tropical landscaping, and the sign code keeps everything understated. It's where you'll find shell shops (She Sells Sea Shells is the biggest), galleries, island-style boutiques, and restaurants. The Island Cow is the go-to breakfast spot (expect a wait). Cielo is upscale Italian. Traders is a local favorite for seafood on the patio. The Sanibel Bean is a walk-up coffee shop that doubles as the island's morning gathering spot.

13. SCCF (Sanibel-Captiva Conservation Foundation)

The SCCF manages over 1,800 acres of preserved land on Sanibel and Captiva. Their nature center on Sanibel-Captiva Road has a native plant trail, a butterfly house, and exhibits on the island's ecology. They run guided nature walks, beach walks focused on marine biology, and turtle-nesting programs in season (May through October — loggerhead sea turtles nest on Sanibel beaches). The SCCF is also the organization that monitors water quality in the surrounding estuaries. If you want to understand the natural side of Sanibel beyond the beach, this is the starting point.

14. Sunset at Blind Pass

Blind Pass is the channel between Sanibel and Captiva at the west end of the island. The beach at Turner Beach (Captiva side) and Blind Pass Beach (Sanibel side) face due west, which means sunset here is unobstructed Gulf horizon. It's one of the most popular sunset-watching spots on the islands, and it also happens to be excellent for shelling — the current through the pass deposits shells on both sides. Bring a beach chair and get there 30 minutes before sunset. The pelicans diving in the pass as the sky turns orange is peak Sanibel.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Sanibel Island worth visiting?

Yes. It's one of the most unique barrier islands in Florida — world-class shelling, a national wildlife refuge covering a third of the island, no high-rises, no chain restaurants. It doesn't look or feel like any other Florida beach destination, which is the whole point.

What is Sanibel Island known for?

Shelling, the J.N. Ding Darling Wildlife Refuge, bike paths, the Sanibel Lighthouse, and a deliberate commitment to low-density development. The island's east-west orientation catches Gulf shells like no other beach on the coast.

When is the best time to visit Sanibel Island?

December through April — warm, dry, and the best shelling (winter storms push shells ashore). February and March are the busiest. November and May are shoulder months. Summer is hot and rainy but prices drop and the island empties out.

Can you still visit Sanibel after Hurricane Ian?

Yes. The causeway is rebuilt, businesses are open, beaches are accessible, the wildlife refuge is operating, and the bike paths are rideable. Some construction continues, but the island is fully welcoming visitors.

How do you get to Sanibel Island?

Via the Sanibel Causeway from the mainland near Fort Myers. $6 toll each way. About 30 minutes from Fort Myers airport (RSW). There's no public transit to the island.
